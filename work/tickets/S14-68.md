## You are on a moonshot

The owner's goal: lift real memory bandwidth on the Xeon Phi 7120P from today's plateau as close as possible to the
physical limit of its memory. This is the base for running LLM decode fast on the card (decode streams every weight
once per token, so it is read-bandwidth bound) and for the owner's later projects (a modern Linux on the card).
Conservative answers are not what we want. Think from the hardware up, question every assumption we made, and bring
wild ideas, as long as each one names its physical mechanism and an experiment that would prove or kill it.

- Best medians, 2 GiB per array, 32 passes, five samples (sweeps 2-3 agree within about 1.3%): read 185-186 (189 with

  about 75 at 120), copy 166. Stencil 328 logical (input reuse, not DRAM traffic).
- Tried without a win: deeper prefetch (16/128, 32/256), unroll 8, split triad, cyclic 1024 with eviction, more threads
  for write/triad/copy, page offsets. Prefetch 8/64 with two threads per core is the read winner.
- GEMV (S11-09): fp32 125, fp16 87, int8 70 GB/s of weight bytes (31/44/70 Gweights/s). The gap to plain read is
  instruction issue, accumulator copies and widening, not DRAM.

- Card: one 7120P (61 cores, 4 threads each, 16 GB GDDR5, MPSS 3.8.6 uOS 2.6.38), host Windows, PCIe Gen2 x4.
  Other KNC models matter too (3120, 5110P): say which ideas carry over.

   result kills it, risk to correctness or the card, and whether it needs the owner's approval.
3. A staged plan: the first card session you would run (at most 90 minutes) and what each outcome decides next.
4. Where the physical ceiling really is for read, write, copy and GEMV-style traffic, and how close you think we can
